## The one thing that changes how you think about all of this

Pinterest is not social media. It's a search engine — closer to Google than Instagram. People don't scroll a feed of accounts they follow; they type something into a search bar and your pin shows up (or doesn't). This means follower count is almost meaningless. One creator with 45 followers and a 4-month-old account was getting 350-600 website visitors *every day*, purely from showing up in searches. 96% of searches on Pinterest are unbranded — people aren't looking for a specific business, they're looking for a solution and open to discovering whoever offers it.

The practical implication: **stop thinking about growing an audience and start thinking about showing up in the right searches.**

## The keyword research process — before you make a single pin

<a name="the-keyword-research-process"></a>

Keywords are the whole game. If your pins aren't targeting what your audience actually types, they're invisible — it doesn't matter how good they look.

**Step 1 — Find out what people actually search for.** Two free methods:
- **Autocomplete**: type your product into the Pinterest search bar and write down everything the dropdown suggests. "Branding kit" might suggest "branding kit for coaches," "branding kit Canva," "branding kit for photographers" — those are real searches.
- **Alphabet soup**: add each letter A-Z after your keyword ("branding kit a," "branding kit b"…) and record what comes up each time. This consistently surfaces 20-30 longtail phrases you'd never think of on your own.

**Step 2 — Find out which of those phrases are actually worth targeting.** Not every search phrase is a good target. Use **PinClicks** (the tool named most often across sources as the best for this): search your phrase, get a scored list, then look at what's *currently ranking* for it. If the top pins are recent and specifically targeting that phrase — it's saturated. If the top pins are old and non-specific — that's your opening. The newsletter walked through this on a real example: "day hike lunch ideas" was rejected because newer pins already dominated it; "day hike first aid kit" was chosen because the top results were old and generic, meaning a fresh, well-targeted pin could outrank them quickly.

**Step 3 — Look at what's winning, so you can beat it.** For your chosen phrase, open the top-ranking pins and note: how many saves they have, when they were pinned, whether they use text overlay or not, what words appear repeatedly in the titles, what colours dominate. This tells you exactly what Pinterest's algorithm thinks "good" looks like for that phrase — then make something better.

**Step 4 — Name your boards after the actual search phrases.** Pinterest uses your board names as signals for how to categorise your pins. Name them after official Pinterest interest categories, not creative names you invented. Use **Interest Explorer** (free, built into Pinterest) to check what the official categories are — search your phrase, and if nothing comes up, broaden it until it does. "Day hike first aid kit" returned nothing; broadening to "first aid kit" revealed "hiking first aid kit" and "hiking first aid kit checklist" as real official categories — so the board gets named that, not something like "Outdoor Essentials."

**For seasonal timing**: use **Pinterest Trends** (free, in your Pinterest account) and set the date range to a full year. Scroll through month by month to see when searches for your topic peak — then create those pins **45-60 days before the peak**, not the week before. Trends that affect Switzertemplates: "new business launch" types spike in January, "rebrand" types in late January and after EOFY, "Instagram templates" have relatively steady demand with spikes around New Year and back-to-business periods.

## What actually gets a pin clicked (not just saved)

<a name="what-makes-a-pin-get-clicked"></a>

These are the things that consistently separate high-click pins from high-save-but-no-click pins:

**Text overlay on the image.** 99% of the top-clicked pins across one creator's analytics had text on them. Without text, pins get saved to mood boards but don't drive traffic.

**An outcome hook, not a feature list.** "Look client-ready by Friday" gets clicked. "1,000+ Canva templates included" gets saved but not clicked. Saves mean someone thought it was pretty. Clicks mean someone thought *I need this*. The outcome has to be specific and slightly urgent — vague benefit statements ("grow your business!") don't convert.

**Lifestyle mockups over flat product shots.** Pinterest's AI now reads the actual image content, not just your keywords. Real-feeling photos — templates shown on a laptop in a styled home office, a Wix site open on a phone in someone's hand — do better than flat screenshot grids or sterile white backgrounds. Not because they look nicer, but because Pinterest can *understand* what they're about.

**The link goes to the product page, not your shop.** One Etsy seller discovered this the hard way: linking to her whole shop produced lots of favourites, zero conversions. The moment she switched to linking each pin straight to the individual product listing, those items started landing in customers' Etsy carts — and kept resurfacing every time that person came back to Etsy. For any Shopify sellers reading this, the advice is even more direct: link straight to checkout, not even the product page, so the customer can pay instantly with Apple Pay or PayPal.

**Spec to match**: 2:3 ratio, 1000×1500px. Keyword in the first 30-40 characters of the title (Pinterest cuts it off on mobile). First 50-60 characters of the description carry the hook — that's what shows before "read more."

**Faces underperform** on most pins — except in fashion, makeup, and jewellery niches. Worth testing whether beauty/wellness coach audiences are the exception for Switzertemplates.

## Two copy-paste pin scheduling recipes

<a name="two-pin-scheduling-recipes"></a>

"Post consistently" is useless advice. Here are two actual recipes that creators are using right now.

### The simple one (good starting point)

1. Pick a product. Make **3 different pins** for it — each with a different photo, a different angle on the hook, and different keywords in the title/description.
2. Write the pin copy by pasting your keywords into ChatGPT and asking for "SEO-driven Pinterest titles and descriptions" — then shorten the output (AI titles always run too long).
3. Schedule them **one per week over 3 weeks** — never post two for the same product on consecutive days, or they compete with each other.
4. For 5 products, that's 15 pins → 5 weeks of consistent coverage, batched in one session.

### The more involved one (better for a content-heavy strategy)

1. After finishing a blog post or product, open **Pinterest Trends** and search the keywords it's built around. Set the date range to 1 year. Scroll down for related keywords Pinterest suggests.
2. Pick **3 keyword sets** from that research: the original post's keywords + 2 more.
3. Make **2 pins per keyword set = 6 pins total**, all linking to the same URL. Each pin has a different text overlay targeting its specific keyword set.
4. Pin to **3 boards**: 1 existing board + 2 *new* boards named exactly after the other two keyword sets. (Generate the board descriptions by giving ChatGPT the target keywords and asking for an SEO-optimised description to copy-paste in.)
5. Schedule one pin per week, rotating: week 1 → board A, week 2 → board B, week 3 → board C, week 4 → back to board A. One product stays "covered" for **5-6 weeks** without touching it again.

Both recipes share the same logic: Pinterest needs to "test" each pin individually — if you post everything at once it gets confused and your pins compete with each other. Spacing gives it time.

## When traffic stalls — the 6 actual causes and fixes

<a name="when-traffic-stalls-the-6-fixes"></a>

One creator framed this clearly: most people think stalled traffic is a design problem. It's not. **It's a signals problem** — Pinterest's algorithm is trying to figure out who you are and who should see you, and something is making that hard. Here's the diagnostic framework (one account went from nothing to 180,000 outbound clicks in 90 days by working through these):

| The problem | What it actually means | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Not enough data | Account too new or posting too rarely — Pinterest has nothing to go on | Pin more, but systematically — not randomly |
| Inconsistent posting | Posting 20 pins one week, 2 the next confuses the algorithm's categorisation | Pick a sustainable floor and hold it *every* week |
| Flat pin titles | No curiosity gap — the reader already knows what you're about to tell them | Rewrite titles around what they *want* to know, not what you have |
| Traffic leak | Visitors landing on your page and leaving in under 10 seconds → Pinterest sees that and penalises your reach | Stronger opening hooks on the landing page + related-product links to keep people moving |
| Guessing keywords | Making pins based on what *you* think they're searching for | Use real search data — PinClicks, Pinterest Trends, the autocomplete method above |
| No feedback loop | Never checking what's working, so you repeat the same mistakes | Review analytics regularly and make more of what's already performing |

## Tools — what they actually do and how to use them

<a name="tools"></a>

- **PinClicks** — keyword research. Searches return scored phrases with data on how much competition exists. Also shows the hidden "annotation" tags on your competitors' top-ranking pins (i.e. the exact categories Pinterest has already decided those pins are about — which tells you exactly what to put in your own description to get the same categorisation).

- **Pinterest Trends** — free, inside your Pinterest account. Shows search volume graphs across any time period. Use the month-by-month scanning method: set the end date to last July, record what's trending, move it to last September, last October, last November, last December — you end up with a full seasonal content calendar mapped out in 20 minutes.

- **Tailwind** — a scheduler. One manager tested Tailwind vs. Pinterest's own native scheduler vs. manually posting live, every day since 2019 — no meaningful difference in reach between any of them. Use whichever creates less friction. Tailwind has a **free keyword tool** where you paste a keyword or a URL and it returns related terms with a "resonance" score (useful when Pinterest won't show you actual search volume for a term). Their **Turbo Pin** beta lets you submit a pin to a shared feed — but you have to engage with 10 other creators' pins first to unlock each submission.

- **Pin Generator** — paste a product link or upload an image, get 10-20 pin variations in minutes, ready to schedule. Useful for hitting a consistent daily floor without designing each one manually.

- **Interest Explorer** — built into Pinterest (search under "Explore" → "Interests"). Use it to find the *official* Pinterest interest category names for your topic — those are the right words to use in board names and pin descriptions.

## Handling algorithm changes without losing your mind

<a name="algorithm-changes"></a>

A creator who's been on Pinterest for 6 years (built a six-figure blog income through Pinterest alone) has a clear three-step response whenever her stats drop:

1. **Wait a week.** 90% of drops self-correct without any action. Pinterest rolls out changes gradually and often reverses them.
2. **If it's still down after a week, change exactly one thing** — not your whole strategy — and give it 3 weeks to show a result.
3. **Never overhaul everything at once.** If you do, you'll never know what was actually working.

She also makes an observation worth remembering: even after she deliberately scaled back her pinning, her monthly page views from Pinterest "barely changed" — still hundreds of thousands a month, on an account she's no longer actively maintaining. That's the compound effect of years of pins still surfacing in searches.

## How long this actually takes, and what it costs

- **Time**: about 1 hour a week. That's the number every source agrees on. Batch pins on a Sunday, schedule for the week, done.
- **When to expect results**: 30-60 days before traffic visibly moves. Most people quit somewhere in months 1-3. Every source independently puts the "flywheel" point — where it starts compounding — at **months 4-6**.
- **If it works**: hand the weekly batch to a VA at ~$30-35/hour. That's roughly $140/month for the full system.
- **Why pins keep working**: average pin lifespan is 4+ months. One creator has pins from 8-11 years ago still driving meaningful traffic. Instagram posts are dead in 24-48 hours. The maths of effort vs. return are completely different.
