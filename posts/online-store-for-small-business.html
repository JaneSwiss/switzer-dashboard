ONLINE STORE FOR SMALL BUSINESS: HOW TO CHOOSE THE RIGHT SETUP FOR WHAT YOU ACTUALLY SELL

The best online store for a small business depends entirely on what you sell and how you sell it. A coach selling digital courses needs a completely different setup than a service provider selling templates or a product-based business shipping physical goods. There is no single "best platform" - only the right platform for your specific business model.

And yet, most advice treats every small business owner the same. You end up reading generic platform comparisons that rank Shopify against Squarespace against WooCommerce without ever explaining which one makes sense for your situation.

Here is the problem with that approach: you pick a platform because it topped someone's list, spend weeks setting it up, and then realize it does not support the way you actually need to sell. Maybe you wanted to offer payment plans but your platform charges extra for that. Maybe you are selling PDFs and the checkout process is clunky. Maybe you are paying monthly fees for features you will never use.

This post breaks down how to choose an online store setup based on your business type, what you are selling, and where you actually are right now. No rankings. No "top 10" lists. Just a clear framework for making a decision you will not regret in six months.

THE DECISION THAT COMES BEFORE EVERYTHING ELSE

Before you compare platforms, you need to answer one question: do you want your own branded storefront, or do you want to sell on a marketplace?

This is not a small detail. It changes everything about how your business operates.

A branded storefront means customers come to your website, see your branding, and buy directly from you. You own the customer relationship. You get their email address. You control the experience.

A marketplace (like Etsy or Amazon) means customers come to a platform where thousands of other sellers also live. You get built-in traffic, but you compete for attention. And here is the part most people do not realize until it is too late: most marketplaces do not give you customer email addresses.

For coaches, consultants, and service providers, this matters enormously. Your business runs on relationships. If someone buys your $47 template on Etsy, you cannot email them about your $497 course unless they happen to find you again. You have made a sale but lost the relationship.

For product-based businesses with physical inventory, marketplaces can make sense as one sales channel. The built-in traffic is real. But treating a marketplace as your only store means building on rented land.

What does this look like in practice?

A business coach selling digital courses and templates should almost always start with their own storefront. The customer list is too valuable to give away.

A handmade jewelry seller might use Etsy for exposure and their own Shopify store for repeat customers and higher-margin sales.

A service provider selling one signature offer (like a VIP day) might not need a full store at all - just a simple checkout tool embedded on their existing website.

The point: make this decision intentionally. Do not default into a marketplace because it seems easier, and do not build a complex storefront when a simple checkout would do.

MATCHING YOUR BUSINESS TYPE TO THE RIGHT PLATFORM

Here is where most guides fail you. They compare platforms feature-by-feature without ever saying who each one is actually built for.

Let me break it down by business type:

If you sell digital downloads only (templates, PDFs, guides, presets):

You need a platform with instant file delivery, not one designed for shipping physical products. Gumroad, Stan Store, Payhip, and Podia all handle this well. They are simple to set up, the checkout is clean, and your customer gets their files immediately.

Shopify can work here too, but you will need to add a digital downloads app, and you are paying for a lot of features you do not need.

If you sell courses or memberships:

This is where Kajabi, Teachable, Thinkific, and Podia shine. They handle video hosting, drip content, community features, and payment plans natively. Trying to bolt course functionality onto a standard e-commerce platform usually creates headaches.

If you sell physical products:

Shopify, Squarespace Commerce, and WooCommerce are all solid choices. Shopify is the most robust for scaling. Squarespace is simpler and integrates beautifully if you already have a Squarespace website. WooCommerce is free but requires WordPress and more technical setup.

If you sell a mix of digital and physical:

Shopify or WooCommerce handle both well. You will need apps or plugins for digital delivery, but the infrastructure supports it.

If you just need to sell one offer (a VIP day, a single service package):

You might not need a full store at all. ThriveCart, SamCart, or even Stripe's payment links can handle single-product checkout beautifully. Embed it on your existing website and you are done.

THE FEES THAT EAT YOUR PROFITS (AND HOW TO CALCULATE THEM)

Nobody talks about this enough: payment processing fees can take 2.4% to 3.5% or more of every sale, depending on your platform and payment gateway.

On a $27 template, that is $0.65 to $0.95 gone. Annoying but manageable.

On a $2,000 coaching program, that is $48 to $70 per sale. Now it matters.

Here is how the fee structures typically work:

  • Platform fee: Some platforms charge a monthly subscription. Others charge a percentage of each sale. A few charge both.
  • Payment processing fee: This is what Stripe or PayPal takes. Usually 2.9% + $0.30 per transaction. Some platforms negotiate slightly better rates at higher volumes.
  • Transaction fee: Some platforms add their own transaction fee on top of payment processing. Shopify charges this unless you use Shopify Payments. Squarespace charges this on their lower plans.

How do you figure out your actual cost per sale?

Take a product you plan to sell. Let us say it is a $197 digital course.

On a platform with no transaction fee, using Stripe (2.9% + $0.30), you pay: $5.71 + $0.30 = $6.01 per sale.

On a platform with a 2% transaction fee plus Stripe, you pay: $3.94 (platform) + $6.01 (Stripe) = $9.95 per sale.

That is a 50% increase in fees for the same product. Multiply by a hundred sales and you have lost nearly $400 to fees you could have avoided.

The takeaway: Before committing to any platform, calculate your expected cost per sale at your actual price points. A platform that seems cheaper at $29/month might cost you more in transaction fees than one at $79/month with no additional fees.

WHAT DIGITAL PRODUCT SELLERS NEED (THAT MOST PLATFORMS GET WRONG)

If you are a coach, consultant, or service provider selling digital products - courses, templates, workbooks, audio files - you need functionality that many mainstream e-commerce platforms treat as an afterthought.

Secure instant delivery. When someone buys a PDF template at 11pm, they should get it immediately. Not "within 24 hours." Not after you manually send it. Immediately. This means your platform needs automated file delivery with download limits and expiration settings to prevent sharing.

Multiple file formats. Maybe your template bundle includes Canva files, a PDF guide, and a video walkthrough. Your platform should handle multiple files per product without making you create workarounds.

License keys or access codes. For software, fonts, or premium templates, you might need unique license keys generated per purchase. Not every platform supports this natively.

Course hosting or integration. If you are selling video courses, you either need a platform that hosts video (Kajabi, Teachable, Thinkific) or a platform that integrates cleanly with a separate course host (Shopify + Teachable, for example).

Payment plans without extra fees. Many coaches sell programs in the $500-$2,000 range. Offering 3-4 payment installments dramatically increases conversions. Some platforms include this; others charge extra or require third-party tools.

A real example of getting this wrong:

I know a business coach who set up her $997 signature program on a basic Squarespace store because her website was already there. The checkout worked, but she had no way to offer payment plans without adding a third-party tool. She also had to manually send course login information to every buyer because Squarespace does not host courses. After three months and forty sales, she migrated everything to Kajabi. She estimates she lost 15-20 sales during the migration chaos.

The lesson: think about what you are selling six months from now, not just today.

SETTING UP YOUR STORE THE RIGHT WAY (STEP BY STEP)

Once you have chosen your platform, here is how to set up an online store that actually converts:

Step 1: Secure your domain first.

Your store needs a professional URL. If your business is "Aligned Life Coaching," your store should be at alignedlifecoaching.com or shop.alignedlifecoaching.com - not alignedlifecoaching.gumroad.com.

Buy your domain through Namecheap, Google Domains, or directly through your platform. Keep it short, easy to spell, and .com if possible.

Step 2: Set up payment processing.

Connect Stripe and PayPal at minimum. Enable Apple Pay and Google Pay if your platform supports them - they significantly reduce checkout friction on mobile.

Understand your fee structure before you launch. Know exactly what each sale will cost you.

Step 3: Create your essential pages.

  • Homepage with a clear value proposition - what you sell and who it is for
  • Shop or products page with organized categories
  • About page (builds trust, especially for service-based businesses)
  • Contact page
  • FAQ page (reduces support requests dramatically)
  • Legal pages: Privacy Policy, Terms of Service, Refund Policy

Step 4: Build product listings that sell.

Write benefit-focused descriptions. "50-page workbook" is a feature. "Finally get clear on your niche so you can stop second-guessing every marketing decision" is a benefit.

Use high-quality images. For digital products, this means professional mockups showing your templates in context. Flat file previews do not sell.

Set clear pricing. Too many options create decision paralysis. One price (or one price plus a payment plan) converts better than five tiers.

Step 5: Configure delivery and fulfillment.

For digital products: test your download links yourself. Buy your own product. Make sure the files arrive, open correctly, and the experience feels professional.

For physical products: set up shipping zones, rates, and carrier integrations. Connect to ShipStation or your platform's native shipping tools.

Set up order confirmation emails. These should include clear next steps - how to access their purchase, what to do if they have questions, and (for digital products) the download link.

Step 6: Connect your email marketing.

This is non-negotiable. Your store should connect to Flodesk, ConvertKit, Mailchimp, or whatever email platform you use.

Set up an abandoned cart email sequence. Someone added your product to their cart but did not complete checkout? Email them within an hour. Then again in 24 hours. This alone can recover 10-15% of lost sales.

Build a post-purchase sequence. A customer just bought - now nurture them toward their next purchase.

Step 7: Set up tax collection.

Yes, this matters. For digital products sold across state or country lines, you may have sales tax obligations. Use your platform's native tax tools or integrate TaxJar or Avalara.

This is not optional. Getting it wrong creates liability. Getting it right takes about an hour of setup.

Step 8: Mobile test everything.

Over 60% of e-commerce traffic comes from mobile devices. Browse your entire store on your phone. Add a product to cart. Complete a test purchase. Check that confirmation emails render correctly on mobile.

If anything feels clunky, fix it before you launch.

THE MOST COMMON MISTAKES (AND HOW TO AVOID THEM)

After watching hundreds of small business owners set up online stores, these are the patterns that consistently cause problems:

Mistake 1: Choosing a platform based on what everyone else uses.

Shopify is excellent - for physical product businesses with inventory. For a coach selling one digital course and a few templates? It is overkill and the monthly fees add up for features you will never touch.

Mistake 2: Ignoring email integration from the start.

Your customer list is the most valuable asset your business will ever have. If your platform does not integrate with your email marketing tool, or if you are selling on a marketplace that does not share customer emails, you are building on sand.

Mistake 3: Underestimating mobile.

Your store might look beautiful on your laptop. But if the mobile checkout requires pinching, zooming, or excessive scrolling, you are losing sales. Test on mobile before anything else.

Mistake 4: No legal pages.

Privacy Policy, Terms of Service, and Refund Policy are not optional. They protect you legally and build customer trust. Use a generator tool if you need to, but have them in place before your first sale.

Mistake 5: Launching without testing the full customer journey.

Buy your own product. Go through every step your customer will experience. Is the checkout smooth? Does the confirmation email make sense? Can you actually access and download what you bought? Find the problems before your customers do.

Mistake 6: Overcomplicating the structure.

You do not need fifteen product categories and a complex navigation menu when you are selling three things. Start simple. Add complexity only when your product catalog genuinely requires it.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

What is the best online store for small business?

It depends on what you sell. For digital downloads, Gumroad or Stan Store offer the simplest setup. For courses, Kajabi or Teachable work best. For physical products, Shopify is the most robust. For a single service or offer, you might only need a checkout tool like ThriveCart embedded on your existing website.

Which online platform is best for small business?

The platform that matches your business model. A coach selling courses has different needs than someone shipping handmade products. Define what you are selling and how you want to sell it first, then choose the platform that supports that specific workflow.

Do I need an LLC to start an online store?

You can legally start selling without an LLC, but forming one provides liability protection - your personal assets are separate from your business. Consult with a local accountant or attorney to understand what makes sense for your situation and location. Many small business owners start as sole proprietors and form an LLC once revenue becomes consistent.

What small business can I start with $5,000?

Digital product businesses have the lowest startup costs - you can launch templates, courses, or downloadable resources for under $500 in platform and tool costs. The remaining budget could go toward branding, a professional website, and initial marketing. Service-based businesses (coaching, consulting, virtual assistance) also require minimal upfront investment compared to physical product businesses.

How much does it cost to run an online store?

Costs range from $0 to $300+ per month depending on your platform and needs. Free tiers exist (Gumroad, Payhip) but often include transaction fees or limitations. Paid platforms like Shopify start around $29/month. Add payment processing fees (2.9% + $0.30 per transaction is standard), any apps or plugins you need, and your domain renewal ($10-20/year). A realistic budget for a simple digital product store is $30-50/month plus transaction fees.

Can I start an online store with no inventory?

Absolutely. Digital products require no inventory - you create once and sell infinitely. Print-on-demand lets you sell physical products (apparel, mugs, prints) without holding stock; products are manufactured and shipped when ordered. Dropshipping works similarly for other product categories. Service-based businesses packaged as products (strategy sessions, audits, VIP days) also need no inventory.

MAKING THE DECISION AND MOVING FORWARD

Choosing an online store for your small business does not have to be complicated. It comes down to three questions: What are you selling? How do you want to sell it? And what does your customer journey look like from purchase to repeat buyer?

If you are a coach or service provider selling digital products, prioritize platforms that handle instant delivery, email integration, and payment plans. If you are building a physical product business, prioritize inventory management and shipping integrations. If you are testing an idea with minimal investment, start with a free or low-cost tool and upgrade once you have validated demand.

The mistake is spending weeks researching and months building the "perfect" store before you have made your first sale. Start simpler than you think you need to. Launch before it feels ready. Learn what your customers actually want, then iterate.

Your online store is infrastructure. It supports your business - it is not your business. The business is the problem you solve, the people you serve, and the products that make their lives better. Get the store functional, then focus your energy on finding and serving those customers.

Pick the platform that matches what you are actually selling today, not the one with the most features you might use someday. You can always migrate later once you know what is working.
