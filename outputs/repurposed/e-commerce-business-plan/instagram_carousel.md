COVER: Your business plan shouldn't collect dust.

SLIDE 1: Most e-commerce business plans are written once and never opened again. That's not a planning problem. That's a template problem. Traditional plans are built for investors — not for you.

SLIDE 2: If you're not pitching VCs, skip the jargon-heavy market analysis and twelve-page financial projections. You need a working document that helps you make decisions. That's it.

SLIDE 3: Start with what you sell and who wants it. But skip the vague descriptions. "High-quality handmade jewelry" tells people nothing. Describe your product in terms of the specific problem it solves.

SLIDE 4: Your target customer isn't a demographic. A 28-year-old in Austin and a 45-year-old in London might both be your ideal buyer. What matters is their situation and motivation — not their age.

SLIDE 5: Get honest about your numbers. What's your average order value? Your margins after fees and shipping? A plan that pretends everything is profitable from day one is useless. A realistic one gives you something to work toward.

SLIDE 6: A useful business plan changes as your business changes. It answers the questions keeping you up at night. It's something you actually open again. That's the goal.

CAPTION:
Every business plan template I found online looked like it was written for a boardroom presentation.

When I was building my own store, I didn't need to impress investors. I needed to get clear on what I was selling, who actually wanted it, and whether the numbers made sense.

If you're starting an online shop — physical products, digital downloads, services — you don't need a complicated plan. You need a clear one. Something you'll actually use to make decisions as you grow.
