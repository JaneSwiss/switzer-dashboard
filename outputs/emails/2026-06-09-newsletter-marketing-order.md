SUBJECT LINE B: The 30-day marketing plan worth actually following
PREVIEW TEXT: Week by week — what to do, how to do it, and why it's in this order.

---

BODY:

If your marketing feels scattered, it's usually not a content problem. It's a sequencing problem. Here's the order that actually works.

---

**Week 1 — Build the foundation**

**Set up analytics first.** Before you post a single thing, connect Google Analytics to your website. It takes about 20 minutes. What to look at: where your traffic comes from, which pages people land on, and how long they stay. You don't need to understand everything yet — you just need the data collecting so you have something real to look at in week three.

**Choose one platform and set it up properly.** Not three. One. If you're deciding where to focus, Pinterest is worth taking seriously — it's a search engine as much as it is a social platform. People go there actively searching for things to buy ("branding kit for coaches," "website templates for therapists") rather than just scrolling. Set up a business account, connect your website, and write a bio using the words your ideal customer would actually type into a search bar — not a description of who you are, but what you help them do.

**Create a lead magnet.** It doesn't need to be complicated. A one-page PDF checklist, a free template, a short how-to guide — anything that gives someone a specific reason to hand over their email address. The best lead magnets solve one small, immediate problem. A "5 things to fix on your website before you launch" checklist takes an afternoon to create and keeps collecting emails for years.

---

**Week 2 — Create the content**

**Write your first blog post.** Target a keyword your customer searches when they have a problem, not a keyword about you. "How to make my business look more professional" is better than "about my design business." Google autocomplete is free — type your topic and see what people are actually asking. Write to answer that specific question clearly and in full.

**Set up a basic email welcome series.** Three emails is enough to start. Email one: deliver what they signed up for, immediately. Email two (2-3 days later): one useful tip relevant to why they joined — no pitch, just value. Email three (a few days after): introduce what you sell, briefly and naturally. Most businesses skip the welcome series entirely, which means new subscribers go cold before they've heard anything useful.

**Build a posting schedule you can actually keep.** Three times a week consistently beats seven times for two weeks and then nothing. For Pinterest specifically: 3-5 pins spread through the day outperforms dropping everything at once — the algorithm rewards consistency, not volume.

---

**Week 3 — Review what's happening**

**Check your analytics.** Three numbers matter right now: where your traffic is coming from, which pages are getting the most visits, and what your email open rate is. If anything surprises you — good or bad — ask why before moving on. One insight from real data is worth more than a month of guessing.

**Test your site on mobile.** Open your website on your phone and click through every page as if you're a customer. Does the text fit without zooming? Do buttons work with a thumb? Can someone fill in your contact form without frustration? More than half your visitors are on mobile — if it's broken there, you're losing them before they've seen what you sell.

**Build a content calendar for the next four weeks.** You don't need to create everything now — just map out one topic per week and which platform it goes to. Blog post, email, and social content can all come from the same core idea. One topic, three formats. That's a sustainable content system.

---

**Week 4 — Refine based on data**

**Look at what actually worked.** Which email got opened? Which pin got saved? Which blog post drove traffic? What did nobody click on? Make one decision based on this — drop what clearly isn't working, do more of what is. One data-backed shift every month compounds over a year.

**A/B test your next email subject line.** Most email platforms (Flodesk included) let you send two subject line variations and automatically pick the winner based on open rate. Test one variable at a time: curiosity vs. benefit, short vs. longer, a question vs. a statement. One test per send. Over time you'll know exactly what your list responds to.

**Plan month two differently to month one.** If Pinterest drove traffic, invest more time there. If a certain type of content got clicks, make more of it. Month two should be built on what you actually learned — not a repeat of what you assumed would work.

---

That's 30 days. Twelve tasks. A marketing setup that's actually built to grow.
